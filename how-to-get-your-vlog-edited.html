How to get your vlog edited (2026)

We run one of the options on this list — a monthly editing service — so read it with that in mind. We have tried to be straight about all seven, including the three that do not involve hiring anybody, and where ours is the wrong answer.

Last updated August 2026 · Written by the team behind Slice of Life

Almost every comparison of this is published by an editing company and compares editing companies. That quietly skips the three options most vloggers actually end up on, and it is why the advice tends to arrive at the same place regardless of who is asking.

There are seven realistic ways to get a vlog edited, and they fail in different ways. Which one is right depends less on budget than on two things: how often you publish, and whether you want to be in the timeline.

The options

Editing it yourself

Best for: If you enjoy editing, or you are still working out what your channel is.

Nothing else gives you this much control, and your own taste is most of why anyone watches you rather than someone else. For the first stretch of a channel it is also how you find out what your edit should feel like, which is not a thing you can brief someone on until you know it yourself.

The catch: The cost is not money, it is the videos that never go up. For a weekly or daily vlogger this is hours per video forever, and it is the step where most channels quietly stop.

Software that assembles it for you

Best for: If the assembling is the part that stops you, and you still want to make the calls.

A newer category, and the one our Mac app sits in. You drop in a day of raw footage and get a draft back — talking-to-camera moments found, your own b-roll cut in underneath, captions on — then you correct it rather than building from an empty timeline. Related tools do narrower jobs: clipping a long video into shorts, or stripping silence and filler out of a recording.

The catch: It is still your hour, just a much shorter one, and you are still the one publishing. It also only works if your footage is the shape the tool expects — ours needs you talking to camera, and gives you very little if you do not.

A freelancer, per video

Best for: One-off videos, or finding out whether handing it over works for you at all.

Post the job, pick from the bids, send the footage. Low commitment, easy to stop, and a genuinely sensible way to test whether you even like receiving an edit you did not make.

The catch: You re-explain your channel every single time, and the person changes. Consistency is the first thing to go, and consistency is most of what a channel's look actually is. Turnaround is whatever that person's week happens to be.

A dedicated freelance editor

Best for: If you publish regularly and want one person who learns your channel properly.

The classic arrangement, and at its best the highest quality option here. The same editor every video starts to know your jokes, your b-roll habits, how your intros run, and stops needing to be told. Find the right person and this is very hard to beat.

The catch: You are now managing someone. Finding them, briefing them, chasing them, and covering the weeks they are ill or away. And when they take a full-time job — which good editors do — you start the search again from nothing.

A monthly editing service

Best for: If you publish on a schedule and want it to keep happening without you managing anyone.

A fixed monthly arrangement: an agreed number of videos, a turnaround someone is accountable for, and staffing that is their problem rather than yours. This is what we do. You send the card, a finished video comes back, and the schedule holds whether or not any one editor is available that week.

The catch: You are buying a set number of videos a month, so it is poor value if you publish in bursts rather than steadily. You also get less frame-level say than briefing your own editor directly.

A production agency

Best for: Brand campaigns, multi-camera shoots, and video that is a company deliverable rather than a personal channel.

Full production around the edit: strategy, shooting, motion design, sometimes distribution, with real project management holding it together. If the video has stakeholders and a sign-off chain, this is the shape built for that.

The catch: It is built for campaigns, not a publishing rhythm. Scoping and approvals cost days before anyone opens a timeline, which is the wrong shape entirely for footage you shot yesterday and want up tomorrow.

An in-house editor

Best for: If you publish daily, or across several channels, and editing is genuinely a full-time job's worth of work.

An employee or a long-term part-timer. Maximum control and maximum availability — they are there when you need a re-cut at nine in the evening, and they know the whole back catalogue.

The catch: Only makes sense at real volume, and you have now hired someone: recruiting, payroll, management, holiday, and a quiet month that costs exactly the same as a busy one.

How we would choose

  • You enjoy editing. Keep doing it yourself. Nothing on this page is an improvement on a part of the job you like.
  • You are in your first stretch of videos and still finding the format. Do it yourself, or use software that gives you a draft. You cannot brief someone on a style you have not settled on.
  • The assembling is what stops you, but you want to stay in control. Editing software that builds a first draft. Our Mac app is free while it is in early access.
  • You publish irregularly, in bursts. A freelancer per video. A monthly arrangement priced against a fixed number of videos is bad value if you skip months.
  • You publish on a steady schedule and want it off your plate. A dedicated editor if you want to manage one, a monthly service if you do not.
  • The video is a company deliverable with sign-off. An agency. That is what the process overhead is for.
  • You are publishing daily across several channels. Hire in-house. At that volume nothing else keeps up.

Where handing it over is the wrong answer

Worth saying plainly, because we would rather you did not waste a month finding out:

  • You are still working out what your channel is. Your first stretch of videos is how you learn what your edit should feel like. Handing that over before you know is buying a consistency you cannot yet describe.
  • Editing is the part you enjoy. Plenty of people find it is the best part of the job. If that is you, this is not a problem that needs solving.
  • You publish in bursts rather than weekly. Any monthly arrangement is priced against videos you are expected to actually send. If you shoot four videos in a week and then nothing for two months, per-video freelance fits you better than we do.
  • You want frame-level control. If you have a view on every cut, you want to be in the timeline — yourself, or directing an editor conversation by conversation. A service will feel like being handed someone else's version of your video.
  • You do not talk to camera. This one is specific to us. Our whole pipeline is built around finding narration and cutting b-roll underneath it. Music-only or pure b-roll footage gives it very little to work with, and a general editor will serve you better.

Common questions

How much does it cost to get a vlog edited?

It depends far more on how you buy it than on the video itself. Per-video freelance work is priced per video, so a long edit with a lot of footage costs more than a short one. Monthly services are priced against a set number of videos a month, so the per-video figure only makes sense once you are actually publishing that often. Ours is priced per finished video, from $995. Whoever you use, the two things that move the number most are the finished length and how much raw footage you send.

How long should it take to get an edited vlog back?

A dedicated editor or a monthly service will usually commit to a turnaround, often one to a few business days. Per-video freelance work runs to whatever that person's week looks like, which is fine for a one-off and hard to build a publishing schedule on. Agencies work in weeks because of scoping and approvals, and the date tends to move. Ours is a week from upload, agreed before we start.

Do I lose creative control if someone else edits my vlog?

Some, and it is worth being honest that this is the real trade rather than the money. You keep the decisions that matter most — what you filmed, what you said, what the video is about — and you give up frame-level choices. If you have an opinion about every cut, you want to be in the timeline yourself or directing an editor directly, and no arrangement on this page will feel right.

What do I need to send an editor?

The raw footage, exactly as it came off the card, and any notes on what the video is about. You should not have to rename files, organise folders or pre-trim anything — if you are being asked to, that work has been pushed onto you. Most arrangements set up a shared folder once and you drop the day's footage into it after that.

Can I try an editing service before committing?

You should insist on it. Ask for one video cut from your own footage before any money changes hands — a portfolio shows you what someone can do with ideal material, not what they will do with yours. We do this as standard for anyone we take on.
